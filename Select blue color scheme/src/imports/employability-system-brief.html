01

Why this exists

The problem is timing, not information

Institutions are not short of data about their students. They are short of a moment at which that data turns into a decision.

Today the sequence runs backwards. A student graduates. Six to twelve months later, a tracer survey goes out. Perhaps 30–40% respond. Someone assembles the results in a spreadsheet, and a report says that employability in a given program was 58%. That report is true, useful for accreditation — and completely useless to the 42%, who left eighteen months ago.

Meanwhile the warning signs were on file the whole time. The falling GPA was in the registrar's system. The missing internship was in the placement office. The disengagement was in the LMS. No one joined them up, because they live in three databases owned by three offices, none of which has a model that says which combinations matter.

Four failures we are fixing

  • Retrospective, not predictive. Outcomes are measured after the intervention window has closed.
  • Fragmented. Academic, engagement, and placement data never meet in one dataset.
  • Undifferentiated. Support is offered to whoever walks in, not to whoever needs it most.
  • Unaccountable. No one can say whether the career workshop, the mentorship scheme, or the internship push actually changed anything.

There is a fifth problem, quieter and more dangerous. The moment you rank students by risk, you have built something that can entrench disadvantage. If the model learns that students from certain districts or backgrounds are less employable, and the institution then invests less in them because the model says they are lost causes, the system has automated the very inequity it was meant to expose. That is why fairness monitoring sits in the core of this build and not in an appendix.

The question the system answers. Not "what was our employability rate last year?" — that is a report. It is: "Of the 1,240 students graduating in 14 months, which 180 are on a trajectory toward unemployment, why, and which of the four things we can actually offer them is most likely to change that?"

02

Scope

What we are building — and what we are deliberately not

The fastest way to kill this project is to let it become a second student information system. It is an analytics layer that sits beside operational systems and reads from them.

It IS

  • A read-mostly analytics platform over copies of institutional data
  • A supervised learning system trained on graduates whose outcomes are known
  • A ranked, explained work queue for career advisors and academic heads
  • An intervention tracker that measures whether support actually worked
  • A fairness auditing instrument over its own predictions
  • A curriculum-evidence engine for program review and accreditation

It is NOT

  • A student information system — it never becomes the record of truth for grades
  • A job board or recruitment marketplace
  • An automatic decision-maker — no student is ever denied anything by a model score
  • A tool students use to see their own risk score (see §09 — this is a deliberate choice)
  • A real-time system — predictions refresh per term, not per click
  • A replacement for advisors; it decides who to see first, never what to say

03

System architecture

Six layers, one direction of travel

Data moves upward only. Nothing in the decision layer ever writes back into a source system, which is what keeps the registrar's office willing to give you access.

Why the layering matters

The read-only boundary. Layer 1 is other people's property. You get a nightly export or a read replica; you never hold a write connection to the registrar's database. This single decision converts a political negotiation ("give my system access to student records") into a technical one ("schedule a nightly extract to this staging server"), and it is usually the difference between getting data and not getting it.

Pseudonymisation happens early, not late. Names and national IDs are stripped at Layer 2 and replaced with a salted hash. Everything above Layer 2 — the entire modelling and analytics estate — works on pseudonymous records. Re-identification happens only at the final presentation step, and only for users whose role and scope permit it. An analyst training a model literally cannot see who the students are.

The fairness gate is structural, not procedural. It is not a dashboard someone remembers to check. It is a step in the deployment pipeline: a model version whose disparity metrics exceed threshold cannot be promoted to active status, and the API that serves predictions only ever serves the active version.

The five defensible claims to uniqueness

01 · Different target

Every serious competitor predicts retention. We predict employment. That is not a tweak — it changes the label source (tracer surveys, not enrolment records), the feature set (internships and work-integrated learning matter enormously; they barely matter for retention), and the horizon (12 months past graduation, not next semester).

02 · Low-data institutions

The incumbents presuppose a data warehouse and a budget. This system's minimum viable input is a registrar's CSV export, a placement spreadsheet, and three years of tracer responses. That is what most universities outside the US and Western Europe actually have.

03 · Fairness as a gate

Not a dashboard — a deployment blocker. Group-wise metrics are computed on every model version, and a version that breaches the disparity threshold cannot be promoted. No competitor in this table ships that.

04 · Closed loop

Prediction → recommendation → logged intervention → observed outcome → retraining signal. Competitors either predict or manage cases. Joining them means the system can eventually answer "does the mentorship programme work?" with evidence rather than testimony.

05 · Curriculum evidence

The same model that scores students, aggregated upward, tells a department which courses and which sequences actually contribute to employability. That converts a student-support tool into an accreditation and program-review instrument for the same build cost.

The honest caveat

Uniqueness here is a combination claim, not an invention claim. Say so explicitly in your defence. Examiners respect "no one has assembled these five things for this context"; they punish "we invented employability prediction."

05

The data

What goes in, and the one thing that can sink you

Before any model: you need a target variable that exists, and features that were knowable before the target occurred. Most student projects fail on the second condition without noticing.

Defining the label

"Employable" is not a measurable thing. "Employed" is. Pin the target down before writing a line of code, because it dictates every downstream choice:

TargetDefinitionTypeSourceUse
employed_12mIn paid work, self-employment, or further study ≥20 hrs/week within 12 months of graduationBinaryTracer survey + alumni DBPrimary target
employed_6mSame, at 6 monthsBinaryTracer surveySecondary — tighter, harder
months_to_first_jobTime from graduation to first employmentContinuous / censoredTracer surveySurvival model, Phase 3
field_relevantJob is related to field of study (self-reported)BinaryTracer surveyQuality-of-outcome measure

The label problem, stated plainly. You can only train on graduates whose outcome you know. If your institution has three cohorts of tracer data at a 35% response rate and 900 graduates per year, your usable training set is roughly 2700 × 0.35 ≈ 945 labelled records. That is workable — but only just, and the 35% who respond are not a random sample of the 100%.

Respondents are biased toward the employed. People with jobs answer surveys about jobs. Your model will be trained on an optimistic sample and will therefore over-predict employment. Address this explicitly: report the response rate, compare respondent and non-respondent demographics, and consider inverse-propensity weighting. Naming this limitation in your defence is far stronger than hoping no one asks.

The leakage rule — read before writing any feature code

Every feature must be computable using only information available on or before the as-of date of the prediction. If you predict at the start of the final year, you may not use final-year grades, the final GPA, the graduation date, or anything from the tracer survey.

Symptom of leakage: your model scores 0.97 AUC.
Reality of this problem: a good model scores 0.72–0.82.
If you see 0.95+, you have leaked. Go and find it.

Sensitive attributes: a specific decision to make and defend

You must decide whether gender, district, and financial-aid status enter the model as features or are held only for auditing. There is no universally right answer, so choose one and justify it:

  • Exclude them from features, retain for auditing. Safer, ethically cleaner, easier to defend. Costs some accuracy, and does not actually guarantee fairness — correlated proxies (secondary school, district-linked course choices) leak the information back in anyway.
  • Include them, and audit hard. Sometimes produces fairer outcomes, because the model can learn that a given GPA means something different under different circumstances. Harder to defend to a review board.

Recommended for this project: exclude from features, retain for mandatory group-wise auditing, and state the proxy-leakage caveat explicitly. It is the defensible default, and the audit is what does the real work either way.

06

Machine learning

Two pipelines: one that learns, one that serves

They run on different schedules, with different inputs, and confusing them is the most common architectural mistake in projects like this.

Why these models and not a neural network

Three reasons, and they are worth being able to recite. Size: with roughly a thousand labelled rows and forty features, a neural network has nothing to learn that gradient boosting will not learn better. Structure: this is heterogeneous tabular data with missing values — precisely where tree ensembles dominate and deep learning underperforms. Explainability: an advisor will be sitting across from a student, and "the model says 0.34" is not an intervention. "No internship, and your GPA has fallen for three semesters" is.

Evaluation: measure the right thing

If 70% of your graduates find work, a model that predicts "employed" for everyone scores 70% accuracy and is worthless. The metrics that matter:

  • Recall on the at-risk class — of the students who genuinely struggled, how many did we flag? This is the metric the institution actually cares about. Missing a struggling student is a far worse error than over-flagging a fine one.
  • Precision@k — if advisors have capacity for 150 cases, how many of our top 150 were genuinely at risk? This maps directly onto real staff capacity.
  • PR-AUC — threshold-independent performance on the minority class.
  • Calibration (Brier score, reliability curve) — when the model says 0.30, do roughly 30% of those students actually end up unemployed? Without calibration, your probabilities are just rankings wearing a decimal point.

Fairness, concretely

Fairness is not one number. Compute per group (gender, district, program, entry route) and per model version:

Demographic parity gap

Difference in flag rates between groups. Large gaps may be legitimate — if one program truly has weaker outcomes, flagging more of its students is correct. Investigate, don't auto-correct.

Equal opportunity gap

Difference in true-positive rate. This one is not negotiable: if the model catches 80% of at-risk men and 55% of at-risk women, it is failing women. Threshold: gap < 0.10.

Calibration by group

Does 0.30 mean the same thing for a rural student as an urban one? If not, the same score triggers different real-world risk, and your thresholds are unfair.

Intervention equity

The neglected one: audit not just predictions but whether flagged students from different groups actually received support at similar rates. A fair model with unfair follow-through is still an unfair system.

07

Core logic

How a term actually runs

The system has one heartbeat: the academic term. Everything else is triggered by it. If you can narrate this cycle from memory, you understand the project.

Three pieces of logic worth specifying precisely

Risk segmentation — thresholds, not tertiles

The tempting implementation is to sort by probability and cut into thirds. Do not. Segment by calibrated probability against fixed thresholds, so that "high risk" means the same thing every term and cohorts stay comparable over time. A reasonable starting point, tuned against advisor capacity:

HIGH    p(employed) < 0.45  → case created, contact within 14 days
MEDIUM  0.45 ≤ p < 0.70  → group intervention, monitored monthly
LOW     p ≥ 0.70          → standard services, no case

Then check the volume the thresholds produce. If HIGH contains 400 students and you have three advisors, the thresholds are wrong for this institution regardless of what the statistics say. Capacity is a real constraint and should be visible in the admin settings, not hard-coded.

Intervention ranking — modifiability, not just magnitude

SHAP tells you which features pushed a prediction down. It does not tell you which of those you can change. Entry qualification score might be the largest negative driver, and it is unchangeable. The recommendation engine must therefore rank by |shap_value| × modifiability_weight × institutional_capacity, where modifiability is a fixed property of each feature:

The what-if engine

Counterfactual scoring, and the most persuasive screen in the product. Take the student's feature vector, flip internship_completed from false to true, re-score, show the delta: "completing a 12-week internship raises this student's predicted employability from 0.34 to 0.58." That is what turns a score into a decision an advisor can defend to a student. Two warnings: this is correlational, not causal, and it must be labelled as an estimate — and you must cap the feature edits to modifiable features only, or someone will inevitably build a screen that says "if this student had been born in a different district."

08

Database

Schema — five zones around one hub

Everything hangs off student, but the student table itself holds almost no personal data. That is deliberate.

Four design decisions in the schema worth defending

  1. Predictions are immutable rows, not a column on student. Every prediction is a historical fact with a timestamp and a model version. You need this to answer "what did we know in March, and did we act on it?" — which is the question an audit or an ethics board will ask.
  2. Features live in a snapshot table with an as_of_date. This is what makes point-in-time correctness enforceable rather than aspirational, and it means training and inference read the same structure.
  3. Demographics are in a separate table from student. A query that builds a feature matrix cannot accidentally include gender because it was sitting in the same row. Access to student_demog is restricted to the fairness auditing service.
  4. user_scope is a table, not a column. One advisor can cover two departments; one head covers a faculty. Modelling scope as rows makes Postgres row-level security policies straightforward and keeps §09 enforceable in the database rather than only in the API.

Career advisor

Exists to: work a prioritised queue of students in their departments and log what was done.

  • See named students in scope, with risk and drivers
  • Open, update and close cases
  • Run what-if scenarios on modifiable features
  • Cannot see other departments
  • Cannot see demographic attributes or the demographic composition of their queue
  • Cannot export lists containing names
  • Cannot change a risk score or override a prediction — only annotate it

Academic head

Exists to: use aggregate evidence to change curriculum and program design.

  • Program and cohort analytics for their faculty
  • Course-level contribution to employability
  • Read-only view of cases in their programs
  • Cannot open or edit cases — that is advisory work
  • Cannot see named students in other faculties
  • Cannot see individual staff-attributable teaching-quality scores (build this and you will create an industrial-relations problem, not an insight)

Data analyst

Exists to: build, evaluate and maintain the models.

  • Full pseudonymous feature matrix across all programs
  • Train, tune, evaluate, propose model versions
  • Generate fairness diagnostics
  • Cannot see any student's name, contact detail or identifier
  • Cannot promote a model alone — requires QA approval
  • Cannot open cases or contact students
  • Cannot query demographics at individual level

QA / ethics officer

Exists to: hold the veto. This role is the reason the system can be defended publicly.

  • Full fairness dashboards and model cards
  • Approve or reject model promotion
  • Read the entire audit log
  • Approve threshold changes
  • Cannot see individual students by name
  • Cannot modify any data, model or case
  • Cannot be the same person as the data analyst — if it is, the control is theatre

System administrator

Exists to: manage accounts, integrations and uptime. Deliberately blind to content.

  • Create users, assign roles and scopes
  • Configure data sources and schedules
  • Monitor jobs, sessions and system health
  • Cannot view any student record, prediction or case
  • Cannot grant themselves an analytical role — role escalation requires a second approver
  • Every admin action is written to the append-only audit log

Student — the hard question

The specification does not list students as users. That is a decision worth making explicitly, because someone will ask.

Recommendation: students never see their own probability or risk tier. A number that says "0.34 chance of employment" is a self-fulfilling prophecy for an eighteen-year-old, and the label sticks harder than the support does.

What they should see: a personal opportunities page — recommended internships, workshops, mentoring — framed as offers, never as a warning. Same underlying engine, no score exposed. Plus, on request, the right to know that predictive analytics is used, and the right to opt out of being scored.

Enforcement, three layers deep. UI hiding is not access control. Enforce at every layer: (1) React routes and components gate on role, so users are not confused; (2) the API re-checks permission and scope on every request, because the UI can be bypassed with curl; (3) Postgres row-level security policies filter by user_scope, so even a SQL injection or a rogue query cannot cross a departmental boundary. Then log every single read of an individual student record to audit_log — who, what, when, why.

Execution

Build order — dependency, not enthusiasm

The instinct is to start with the model, because it is the interesting part. Start with the data, because without it the model is a demo on synthetic rows and everyone can tell.

The order that kills projects

Building the UI first. It feels productive, it demos well in week four, and then the model arrives in week sixteen and every screen has to change because the actual output shape is nothing like the mock. Build the pipeline, get one real prediction on one real student, and only then design around what the system genuinely produces.

The fallback plan you should prepare now

If institutional data access is refused or delayed past Phase 1 — which happens more often than not — you need a rehearsed answer. In order of preference: (a) a partner institution or a single cooperative department; (b) a public graduate-tracer dataset from a national statistics office, which sacrifices institutional specificity but preserves methodological rigour; (c) a documented synthetic dataset generated from published employability statistics, with the generation process itself treated as a contribution and its limitations stated in every result. Option (c) is defensible only if you say clearly and repeatedly that it is synthetic. Passing off synthetic results as real is the one mistake with no recovery.

Honest limitations

What can go wrong, and what to say about it

A limitations chapter that names real problems is stronger than one that names none. Every item here should appear in your final write-up.

RiskLikelihoodWhat it does to youMitigation
Data access refused or delayedHighProject stalls at Phase 1; no labels, no modelStart the request in week 1. Prepare the three-tier fallback. Offer to work on a single department first.
Too few labelled recordsHighModel overfits; confidence intervals become embarrassingly widePrefer simple models, use cross-validation honestly, report intervals, do not claim precision you do not have.
Tracer non-response biasHighModel trained on the employed; systematically over-optimisticReport response rate, compare respondent demographics, apply propensity weighting, state the bias plainly.
Target leakageMediumSpectacular offline metrics, worthless real predictionsEnforce as-of dates in the snapshot table. Treat any AUC above 0.90 as a bug report, not a result.
Labour market drives the outcome, not the studentHighAn economic shock makes the model wrong for everyone at onceInclude cohort/year effects, monitor drift, retrain annually, and never present the model as a forecast of the economy.
Proxy discriminationMediumExcluded attributes re-enter through correlated featuresAudit on the attributes you excluded. Test whether they are recoverable from your features — if they are, say so.
Labelling and self-fulfilling prophecyMediumStudents treated as low-potential become low-potentialNever expose scores to students. Frame outputs as offers of support. Audit whether flagging changes staff behaviour negatively.
Resource reallocation away from the flaggedMediumThe model becomes a reason to give up on peoplePolicy commitment, written into the deployment agreement: risk flags may only increase support, never reduce it.
Advisors ignore the queueHighTechnically perfect system, zero adoptionCo-design with advisors from Phase 0. Fit their existing workflow. Measure queue completion as a system KPI.
Correlation read as causationHighInstitution mandates internships for everyone and sees no gainLabel what-if outputs as estimates. Recommend a pilot with a comparison group before any policy change.
Scope creep across 14 modulesHighFourteen half-built modules instead of six working onesFreeze Phase-1 scope in writing. Ship M09/M10 as instrumented-but-unpopulated, labelled honestly.

The sentence to build the defence around. "This system does not predict who deserves support. It predicts who is likely to need it, explains why in terms a person can act on, records what was done, and audits itself for whether it treats groups of students equally."

Everything in this document — the pseudonymisation boundary, the fairness gate, the separation of duties, the decision not to show students their own score — exists to make that sentence true rather than merely stated.

Where to go next

Three things, in this order. Confirm the label source — find out this week whether your institution has tracer-survey data and how many cohorts. That single fact determines whether this is a modelling project or a data-collection project. Write the target definition in one paragraph and get someone in career services to agree with it. Then draw the schema from §08 in a real migration file and start ingesting one source.
